Pick a key to see where the scale sits on the neck and which chords belong to it.

?Root: the note the scale starts on and comes home to.
Scale: the set of notes that sound right around that root. Root and scale together name the key.
?Circle of fifths: all twelve keys, each a fifth (seven frets) above the one before it, going clockwise. Outside are the major keys; inside each is its relative minor, which uses the same notes. The tinted neighbours differ from the current key by a single note.

?Position: a stretch of the neck where you can play the whole scale without moving your hand.
CAGED: five positions, each around one open-chord shape (C, A, G, E, D) of the key's home chord moved up the neck. That chord's notes are ringed.
3 notes per string: seven positions with the same count on every string. Even picking, wider stretches. ?Degree: a note's place in the scale, counted from the root. 1 is the root, 3 and 5 the third and fifth notes of the major scale; b3 is the 3rd lowered by one fret, #4 the 4th raised by one. Degrees are the same in every key, so a shape learned in degrees works anywhere on the neck.

?The position on the neck, or the chosen progression, written as tab: each line is a string, lowest at the bottom, and each number the fret to play.

?The chords made only from this scale's notes: start on each note and take every other one (root, 3rd, 5th).
Roman numerals say which degree a chord starts on: capitals for major, lower case for minor, ° for diminished (minor with a lowered 5th), + for augmented (major with a raised 5th). A progression written in numerals, like I – V – vi – IV, can be played in any key.
